Slow-Motion Timer

A millisecond timer designed to be read back from high-FPS phone footage.

Overview

This project is a simple millisecond timer built around a microcontroller and a TM1637 four-digit display, designed specifically to be read in phone slow-motion footage. The idea is straightforward: you start the timer at the first reference point, stop it at the second, and then review the footage frame-by-frame to get an accurate time between those two events.

It’s especially useful for quick experiments where you don’t want to wire up sensors or write a full data logger — things like estimating acceleration over a short distance, timing a launcher or mechanism, or comparing how long different setups take to spin up.

Indoors under stable lighting, the timer is very easy to read from slow-motion footage. In bright sunlight, however, the phone’s automatic exposure and very short shutter speed can sometimes “miss” segments or catch the display mid-refresh. The result looks like the timer is less clear outside, but it’s actually a camera sampling and shutter interaction issue rather than a problem with the timer logic itself.

How it works

The timer uses a momentary button and a simple state machine:

  • On the first button press, the timer resets to zero and starts counting in milliseconds.
  • On the second press, it stops and holds the final time on the display.
  • You then film the whole event in slow motion (e.g., 240 fps) with the display in frame.
  • Later, you scrub the footage frame-by-frame and read the stopped value directly on the display.

Because it’s just a basic 4-digit display, the practical range is 0–9999 ms — perfect for short launch, drop, or actuation events where you care about sub-second timing without over-engineering the setup.

Parts used

  • Microcontroller: Arduino-compatible board (e.g. Arduino Nano / Uno).
  • Display: TM1637 4-digit 7-segment display module.
  • Input: Momentary push button for start/stop.
  • Power: USB or 5 V supply (e.g. from your laptop or a power bank).
  • Misc: Breadboard or perfboard, jumper wires, basic header pins.

Indoor vs sunlight behaviour

The timer worked almost perfectly indoors: room lighting and a slightly longer effective shutter time meant every frame captured a clean, stable view of the digits. In direct sunlight, the phone ramps up shutter speed and adjusts exposure on the fly, which means:

  • Some frames are captured mid-refresh while the TM1637 is multiplexing the digits.
  • Exposure changes and reflections can make certain segments appear dimmer or “missing”.

The underlying timing is still correct — the microcontroller keeps solid millisecond counts — but the camera occasionally samples during those brief refresh windows. For serious outdoor measurements, the next step would be a higher-brightness display or a camera with manual exposure and shutter control.
